Current State Assessment Framework

Before the roadmap: where exactly is productivity getting lost today?

Any PE-style or CEO-level transformation starts here. Not with the solution - with a structured, honest diagnostic of where time, money, and quality are being lost right now. This framework gives you the questions to ask, the data to collect, and the picture to build before a single tool is deployed.

The 5 diagnostic lenses

Five ways to see where the organisation is bleeding.

Each lens answers a different version of the same question. Run all five across your 1,200 people before the roadmap is finalised.

⏰
Time audit
Where does a working day actually go? Ask each role to log time by activity category for two weeks. The results are almost always a surprise to leadership.
🛠
Process friction map
Where do handoffs break down? Where do people wait? Where do they redo work because requirements were unclear? Map the flow of a story from brief to production.
📊
Metric baseline
Pull the numbers that exist today: PR cycle time, MTTR, story rejection rate, test coverage, onboarding ramp time. These become the before in every before-and-after.
🗣
Qualitative interviews
30-minute conversations with 2 people from each role. Ask what they find frustrating, what they redo, what they wish they could stop doing. The answers surface pain that no dashboard shows.
👁
Observation sessions
Shadow one developer, one QA, one PM, one BA for a morning each. Watch what they actually do - not what they report they do. The gap between the two is where the biggest wins live.
Role-by-role diagnostic

What to look for in each role.

Each role has a distinct set of productivity drains. These are the specific things to investigate in your organisation - not assumptions, but questions to answer with real data.

Where time is lost
Boilerplate and scaffoldingWriting repetitive code patterns that AI can generate in seconds. Estimated at 20-35% of coding time in most orgs.
Context switchingSwitching between a PR review, a Slack message, and the feature they were building. Each switch costs 15-20 minutes of re-immersion time.
Answering onboarding questionsSenior engineers interrupted by new joiners asking questions a good knowledge base would answer. Track how many Slack messages senior devs receive per day.
Writing tests manuallyUnit and integration tests written by hand for code that already exists. Most orgs have large coverage gaps not because devs do not care but because test writing is slow.
Questions to ask developers
"What did you do in the last two days that felt like a waste of your skills?"
"How long does a typical PR take from open to merged? What causes the delays?"
"If you join a new service or codebase, how long before you feel confident making changes?"
"How often do you push back on a story because requirements are unclear?"
"What is the one process you would eliminate if you could?"
Metrics to baseline now
PR cycle time
Average hours from PR open to merge. Pull from GitHub. Industry benchmark: 1-2 days for healthy teams.
Deployment frequency
How often does each team deploy to production? Low frequency is often a symptom of slow PR review or insufficient test coverage.
Onboarding ramp time
Days until first independent PR merged. Ask EMs. High ramp time signals poor knowledge transfer infrastructure.
Story rejection rate
% of stories pushed back at sprint planning for unclear requirements. Track in Jira for 4 weeks.
Where time is lost
Manual regression cyclesRunning the same test scenarios by hand every release. In most orgs this consumes 40-60% of QA capacity per sprint.
Writing test cases from scratchAuthoring test cases for features that already have acceptance criteria. The criteria exist - translating them to test cases manually is the waste.
Reactive defect triageFinding bugs after they reach production rather than before. Each production defect costs 10-100x more to fix than a pre-release defect.
Coverage gaps no one mapsServices and features with no test coverage that everyone knows about but no one has time to address systematically.
Questions to ask QA engineers
"What percentage of your week is manual regression? Is that higher or lower than 6 months ago?"
"Which services do you worry most about before every release? Why?"
"How often do bugs escape to production that you feel should have been caught in QA?"
"How long does a full regression run take? What could you do if that time was freed up?"
Metrics to baseline now
Test coverage by service
Pull from SonarQube or equivalent. Map which services are below 60% and which have zero coverage.
Defect escape rate
% of bugs found in production vs pre-release. Higher escape rate = QA is under-resourced relative to release velocity.
Manual test cycle time
Hours per sprint spent on manual regression per QA engineer. Self-reported is fine for a baseline.
Flaky test count
Tests that pass and fail intermittently. Flaky tests erode trust in the entire CI pipeline and slow down every deploy.
Where time is lost
Status reporting and aggregationBuilding weekly status decks by aggregating data from Jira, Slack, and email. Ask PMs to time-track this for one week - the number is usually 4-8 hours.
Meeting overheadAttending standups, refinements, retros, stakeholder syncs, and leadership reviews. How many of these require the PM to be live vs could be async summaries?
Roadmap re-planningRebuilding roadmap slides every time priorities shift. The underlying data exists in Jira - the re-presentation is manual and repetitive.
Stakeholder chasingFollowing up on decisions, approvals, and feedback that are blocking sprint progress. Track how many Slack messages are chasing a response.
Questions to ask PMs
"How many hours last week did you spend producing reports or updates rather than making product decisions?"
"Which recurring meetings could you replace with an async summary without losing anything important?"
"How much time do you spend chasing decisions that are blocking your sprint?"
"When was the last time you had a full day to think about product strategy rather than process management?"
Metrics to baseline now
Hours per week on reporting
Ask PMs to log it for 2 weeks. Self-reported but directionally accurate enough to baseline.
Sprint predictability
% of stories committed at sprint start that are delivered by sprint end. Jira data, last 8 sprints.
Meeting hours per week
Pull from calendar data. Focus on recurring meetings vs one-off decisions.
Decision lag time
Average days between a decision being needed and it being made. Often invisible until you start tracking it.
Where time is lost
Story writing from scratchWriting the same user story structure - context, acceptance criteria, edge cases, DoD - for every feature. The format is always the same. Only the content changes.
Requirements clarification loopsStories going back and forth between BAs and developers because the requirements were ambiguous. Each loop costs a sprint day minimum.
Stakeholder brief processingTurning a 30-minute stakeholder conversation into a structured brief and set of stories. Most of this translation work follows a repeatable pattern.
Cross-referencing existing workChecking whether a new requirement overlaps with an existing feature, story, or epic. This is a search and summarisation task that AI can do in seconds.
Questions to ask BAs
"How long does it take you to write a user story from a stakeholder brief to dev-ready?"
"How often do developers come back to you during a sprint because requirements were unclear?"
"What part of story writing feels most repetitive - the part that follows the same structure every time?"
"How do you currently check if a new requirement has already been built or is in progress somewhere?"
Metrics to baseline now
Time per user story
Ask BAs to time 10 consecutive stories. From stakeholder input to dev-ready in Jira. The number is usually 30-60 minutes.
Story rejection rate at planning
% of stories pushed back by developers for unclear requirements. Track in Jira for 4 sprints.
Mid-sprint clarification rate
How often developers return to BA during a sprint for requirements clarification. Each interruption is a lost hour on both sides.
Stories per BA per sprint
Output capacity. Combine with time-per-story to calculate the ceiling of what is currently possible without AI.

Before we go further

Let us be precise about what this is.

This programme is
  • A structural change to how 1,200 people work every day
  • An investment in the people already here, not a replacement for them
  • A compounding asset. The internal copilot copilot trained on your codebase gets more valuable every month
  • A response to waste that is measurable today if you baseline it
  • A managed rollout with governance, security review, and phase-gate measurement
This programme is not
  • A headcount reduction exercise. It is a capacity expansion exercise
  • A shortcut around fixing broken processes. AI amplifies whatever process exists
  • A one-time tool deployment. Adoption requires sustained change management
  • A top-down mandate. Adoption is driven by peer proof, not executive instruction
  • A replacement for senior engineering judgment. AI assists, engineers decide
Guiding Principles

Four principles that do not bend.

Principle 01
Human in the loop, always
Every AI-generated artefact passes through a human review gate before it touches production. AI accelerates. Humans decide. No exceptions.
Principle 02
Peer adoption over mandate
Adoption is driven by champions showing genuine wins to colleagues, not by executive decks or compulsory training. Engineers trust engineers.
Principle 03
Outcomes, not usage
We track PR cycle time, defect escape rate, sprint velocity, and MTTR. Not login counts. Usage metrics are proxies. Outcome metrics are the point.
Principle 04
Security by design
IP protection, DLP controls, and vendor contract review are first-class requirements. No tool deploys without clearing our security review process.

Risk Register

Five risks. Five mitigation strategies.

Risk management is not about avoiding risk. It is about knowing which risks you are taking, monitoring the early warning signals, and having a response ready before you need it.

R1 IP leakage and data security breach
Critical

Root cause: Developers pasting proprietary code into unapproved LLMs; shadow AI usage; vendor training on submitted code.

Mitigation: Copilot Business contract blocks training on your code. DLP (Nightfall) monitors outbound API calls. Mandatory security briefing before access. Only approved AI endpoints reachable from corp devices.

Early warning: Unapproved tool access attempts above 5/week in proxy logs.

R2 Adoption collapse below 40% active use
Critical

Root cause: No quick wins in first 2 weeks; leadership not visibly using AI; one-size onboarding; CoE defunded at month 4.

Mitigation: Week 1 onboarding delivers visible value in the tool itself. CTO uses AI on-screen in next all-hands. CoE budget locked for 18 months not moveable.

Early warning: WAU below 40% at day 30. Teams with zero usage for 2 consecutive weeks.

R3 AI-generated defects reaching production
High

Root cause: Developers accepting suggestions without understanding them; AI writing logically wrong edge case handling; AI-written tests that test the wrong thing.

Mitigation: "AI-assisted" PR label triggers higher reviewer scrutiny. CodeRabbit added to pipeline. Test coverage threshold enforced in CI. Training module: how to review AI code.

Early warning: Production incident rate above 25% increase 60 days post-rollout.

R4 Vendor lock-in and concentration risk
Medium

Root cause: Deep integration into one vendor's IDE; internal copilot built on one model with no abstraction layer; negotiating power disappears once the org is fully committed.

Mitigation: Internal RAG copilot built on model-agnostic API layer. Tested fallback (Tabnine on-prem). 2-year contract maximum, no auto-renew. Annual benchmark of new entrants.

Early warning: Single vendor above 60% of AI spend triggers diversification planning.

R5 Skill atrophy: developers losing core engineering ability
Medium long-term

Root cause: Junior developers never learning to write code from scratch; debugging skills degrading; architectural thinking outsourced to AI over 2–3 years.

Mitigation: Junior engineer "AI-off" sessions: 20% of sprint work done without AI. Debugging curriculum updated: dev must explain root cause before merge. Architecture reviews: senior engineer authors first draft of any ADR. Annual foundations check for L1–L3.

This is the risk most organisations underestimate. It is slow, invisible, and by the time it becomes visible it has already set the organisation back 2–3 years.

Problem Prioritisation

How to define which problem to solve first

You do not decide based on instinct. You decide based on four criteria scored by the people closest to the work, then constrained by the dependency map below. The first problem you solve needs to produce a visible win within 6 weeks that win funds political capital for everything that follows.

Score each problem across four questions
Impact
How badly is this problem hurting the org right now?
Score 1 to 5. A score of 5 means real money or quality is being lost every sprint and everyone knows it.
Speed to proof
How fast can you show a measurable result after deploying?
Score 1 to 5. A score of 5 means visible impact within days of going live, not months.
Reach
How many people does solving this actually affect?
Score 1 to 5. A score of 5 means it affects most of the 1,200 people in scope, not a small specialist team.
Deployment complexity
How hard is it to actually deploy the solution?
Inverted: score 5 means a plug-in tool exists today. Score 1 means a 6-month custom build with significant prerequisites.

🧮 How these numbers are calculated
▼
Loaded hourly rate
Annual cost ÷ 2,080 working hours
2,080 = 52 weeks x 40 hours. This converts your annual loaded cost into an hourly rate.
Cost per second
(Headcount x Hours/day x Hourly rate) ÷ (8 hours x 3,600 seconds)
Divides the daily cost of lost hours across every second of an 8-hour working day.
Running total
Seconds elapsed since start date x Cost per second
If no date is entered, counts from the moment you filled in the inputs. Past date = fixed total since then. Future date = projected loss until then.
Annual cost
Daily cost x 220 working days
220 = 52 weeks x 5 days minus public holidays. Adjust if your org has a different working calendar.
What this represents
This is the cost of time spent on manual work that AI can automate. It does not represent total payroll cost — only the fraction of each person's day that is recoverable. The accuracy of this number depends entirely on the accuracy of your "hours lost per day" input. Start conservative.

🧮 How the timeline is calculated
▼
Champion cohort size
max(20, total headcount x 2.5%)
Minimum of 20 people. Capped at 2.5% of total headcount. This is the initial group given tools and space to find genuine wins before wider rollout.
Culture multiplier
Fast = 0.8x  |  Structured = 1.0x  |  Conservative = 1.35x
Applied to every phase duration. A fast-moving culture moves through each wave faster. A conservative culture needs more change management time between waves.
Maturity multiplier
Partial = 0.85x  |  Some = 0.92x  |  None = 1.0x
Applied on top of the culture multiplier. Orgs with existing AI experience move faster through early phases because the learning curve is shorter.
Phase durations
Base weeks x culture multiplier x maturity multiplier
Base durations: Champions = 3 weeks, Early adopters = 8 weeks, Full rollout = 14 weeks, Culture shift = 18 weeks. All adjusted by the two multipliers above.
Adoption target
Fast culture = 82%  |  Structured = 75%  |  Conservative = 65%
Percentage of people using AI tools at least once per week by month 18. Conservative orgs are modelled with a lower ceiling due to higher change resistance.
